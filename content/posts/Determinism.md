The answer to the title questions is an obvious one, you think, as you mull it over in your mind. But why are you thinking about whether you're the author or narrator of your thoughts at all? Without the prompting of the question, would that be something you would even be thinking about?

When having a conversation, how much of what you say is pre-planned and pre-written in your mind, and how much just... comes out? I'd imagine it's quite heavily in favour of the latter, even if you have pre-arranged ideas of *what* you'd like to talk about. This phenomenon can be more pronounced with THC but it's also noticeable by observing it in normal conversation too. 

There's a scene from series 1 of Westworld that has always stuck with me. One of the lead characters, Dolores, repeats the same morning routine day after day as part of her programming as one of the robots in the park. At one point she asks someone "Have you ever questioned the nature of your reality?" This question had been asked to her many times by one of the park engineers, but she hadn't yet asked herself it. We're aware of many theories of consciousness from the religious to the material, but have we ever asked *ourselves* the question seriously?

How many times have you opened the bathroom cupboard to grab the toothpaste when knowing that it ran out yesterday and you've not replaced the tube? Are you consciously deciding to reach for it, or are you observing yourself perform a rote action in real time? The feeling of surprise when you've gone to reach for something you *know* isn't there would indicate the second.

There's also the phenomenon of seeing a post as you scroll through your timeline that prompts a thought of 'oh I should look into X'. As you scroll on you immediately lose what it is you just thought to yourself you need to look into. It's not until you scroll back up and see that original post that the thought comes back again. The old adage of retracing your steps to find a lost item seems grounded in this truth that our environment produces our thoughts.

## Beyond mere curiosity
These examples aren't merely just curious observations. They point to a much deeper truth of what our felt reality actually is. We've already covered conversations, motor action and forward planning. These domains we typically think of as very deliberate parts of our experience but the evidence does not seem to support that view.

This topic isn't solely a philosophical novelty or plaything either, it has far reaching consequences for the view of the world in which we live.

Though, on the topic of philosophy, it's probably worth clarifying the point I am making and to clear up any confusion. I am not saying that the world and all actions within it are pre-planned or written in the books of fate (neatly called 'fatalism'). No what I am outlining is that of determinism, the idea that actions occur in response to inputs and causes. In particular I am considering the limited case that our *conscious experience* is deterministic, not necessarily that of the whole world (though a strong case could be made for that too.)

## Some other questions 
Of consideration are also some other seemingly trivial things like the subjects artists paint. Why is it that J.M.W. Turner, the visionary and futuristic Victorian artist, painted steam trains and not aeroplanes? Was his imagination not that good, or was he just reflecting the world that he saw around him? 

Why does most sci-fi of the post-war 20th century imagine a future world with a unified (often oppressive) global government or opposing all-powerful states? Was that the extent of all possible futures imagined, or was that just their own Cold War world being projected forwards? 

These may sound inconsequential but I think they continue to reveal an insight into the underlying workings of the human mind.

## How we view the world
It's fair to say we are often not aware of the underlying forces that shape our view of the world.  It comes as a surprise to many, and often too late (yes I'm talking about myself here), how being hangry completely rewrites how you see the world and interactions in it. It's not until you've eaten and you feel your mood shift that you're aware how that thing that really got under your skin is really no big deal and easily dealt with. Similarly, it would be naive to say that exogenous chemicals don't influence and change your view of the world. 

What these examples point to is not the secondary school understanding that we're a soup of chemicals and hormones, but that our perspective of ourselves from within that system is one that can be obfuscated in such a way that we presume ourselves to be seeing the world clearly and rationally, but in fact we are affected in ways we are not aware of.

Another example is that of how we act and behave around different people. Some people bring out the best in us, we're effortlessly eloquent and funny around them, but around others we find ourselves tripping over our words or putting our foot in our mouth. I'm less interested in the reasons for *why* that happens (is it body language, smell, status etc.) and more interested in the fact that it happens without our control or knowing. It just happens - we're a passenger. 

Now to take that idea towards what I'm getting to is that the conscious experience we have is composed of myriad factors, influences and processes that we are not privy to whice have the effect of making it seem as if we are deciding things deliberately, but for which we are merely the last in the chain to get the message.

Think of many times in your life, most particularly as a child, but if we're being honest even as adults, when someone asks you why you did something. You tend to stand there, slightly taken aback and realise that, really, you don't know.

"Why did you hit your brother?" 

"Truthfully, I couldn't tell you, I just did". 

The whole literature regarding the hemispheric differences within our brain provide ample evidence for the *post-hoc* rationalising nature of our consciousness with the left hemisphere playing a particularly important role [^1]. The notion that we conceive a plan and then act it out is seemingly not surported by the evidence.

## Modern influences on this view
It would be perhaps comically un-self-aware to not investigate what factors have led me to believe this worldview accurate. 

Consciousness is perhaps my main interest and one which seems magical within the context of the physical world. The way we understood consciousness throughout history is fuelled by the technology, and hence our metaphors used to describe it. In our recent past the mind and body have gone from clockwork mechanmisms with muscles being analogous to springs and pullies; a steam engine where pressure must be released from the system (think Freud); to more recently CPUs processing the information entering our nervous system and rendering an image upon the screen of our eyes. 

These metaphors have been very much physically based with the experience of *how* we reason being left up for debate. The computers on which our most recent metaphors had been based where good at number crunching but not at thought and reasoning.

However, with the advent of large language models (LLMs) in recent years, this too has fallen. LLMs have shown that human level reasoning and speech is possible through statistical inference. Whilst their achievements, training, refinement and deployment are nothing short of extraordinary, the meme of them being linear algebra on steroids does at least point in the right direction.

The concept of our conscious experience being so easily mimiced by just statistical inference is a real kick to the concept of free-will. 

## How this can be useful
Anything that takes away human autonomy or the concept of free-will is going to be unpopular and normally backfire (more evidence for prompt-response if you will)[^2]. But can the ideas we've briefly covered provide something useable in day to day life?

Well the environment in which we're in seems to play a crucial role to our experience whether that's the macro environment of who you surround yourself with or the micro environment of which post you last saw on the timeline. 

In short it can be summarised as this: you can't choose your feelings but you can choose your environment. The next logical question considering what we have just been discussing about determinism is: Where does the desire to change your environment come from? Put inelegantly, presumably from the cascade of feelings that the current environment gives you which leaves you feeling like you want a change[^3].

## Ancient insights
Whilst the advent of LLMs has provided much needed evidence for this view of the world, these insights are by no means new. Taoism for example makes reference to the flowing and ungraspable nature of reality. There's an underlying view that reality cannot be changed or even fully understood. To our Western minds this is defeatist and passive, 'of course we can understand and shape the world and our destiny'. However the view outlined here goes some way to providing some support for the Taoist view. 

The view that we are merely observers of our experience unfolding before us also chimes with the Buddhist insight of no-self and detachment. We have the illusion of being a unified self but this is merely that, an illusion. 

In summary then: We are pushed around by forces outside of our control and whilst we feel that we are making rational and logical choices, we are in fact simply responding to the prompts that the world gives us.
